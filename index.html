The four voicing types

Closed triads put root, 3rd and 5th on three adjacent strings — the tightest possible stack, twelve grips per chord (4 string sets × 3 inversions). Open (spread) triads drop the middle voice an octave, skipping a string for a wide, ringing sound. Drop-2 takes a four-note seventh chord and drops its second-highest voice an octave — the classic playable 7th-chord grips on four adjacent strings. Shells strip a seventh chord to root, 3rd and 7th — the 5th adds nothing to the quality, so jazz players skip it.

Inversions are named by the bass

Whatever chord tone is lowest names the inversion: root in the bass is root position, 3rd in the bass is 1st inversion, 5th is 2nd, and (for sevenths) 7th in the bass is 3rd. This holds for spread and drop-2 shapes too, even though they're derived by reordering a closed stack.

How to practice

Pick one chord, one string set. Play through the three inversions ascending the neck until the shapes connect into one mental map. Then start the metronome slow (60 or below), let it push you between grips, and only speed up when the changes are clean. Scale Run mode does the same thing through all seven chords of a key — the fastest route to actually using triads in songs. Arrow keys step voicings manually; turn Sound on to link each grip to its sound.

Anchors first, not everything at once

Start with the naturals on the low E and A strings — that's Level 1, and it's where barre-chord roots live, so every hour invested pays immediately. Notes sit two frets apart except the two seams where they're neighbors: E–F and B–C.

Octave shapes do the rest

Every note on the D and G strings is an octave shape away from an anchor you already know (two strings over, two frets up); crossing onto the B or high-E string stretches the shape to three frets. Working out a note via its anchor is not cheating — it's the skill. It just gets fast until it feels like recall.

Use the machinery

Questions are weighted toward what you miss, so trust the quiz to find your gaps. When accuracy is comfortable at a level, move up a level — and when you're accurate but slow, turn the timer on and let it squeeze your recall time down. Speed is the real goal: on stage, "eventually" is a wrong answer.

On the fretboard, intervals are shapes

Same string: the interval is just frets apart. One string up: subtract 5 frets (4 when crossing onto the B string). So a perfect 5th is one string up, two frets over; a major 3rd is one string up, one fret back. The sight modes here build exactly that geometry — drill them until distance is something you see, then use the ear modes until it's something you hear.

Why numbers instead of chord names?

Both Roman numerals and Nashville numbers describe a chord by its scale degree — its position in the key — instead of its letter name. The payoff is that a progression becomes transposable: 1–4–5 is C–F–G in the key of C, G–C–D in the key of G, E♭–A♭–B♭ in E♭. Learn the numbers once and you know the progression in every key. This is how Nashville session players read charts and how musicians call tunes on the bandstand.

Roman numerals (the classroom system)

Quality is shown by letter case: uppercase is major (I, IV, V), lowercase is minor (ii, iii, vi), and the diminished chord gets a degree sign (vii°). In C major: I ii iii IV V vi vii° = C Dm Em F G Am Bdim.

Nashville numbers (the working system)

Plain Arabic numbers, always counted from the major scale of the key. Major is unmarked; minor gets a dash (6- is the vi chord); diminished gets °; sevenths are added as superscripts (5⁷ dominant, 1Δ⁷ major 7, 2-⁷ minor 7). In C major: 1 2- 3- 4 5 6- 7°.

In a minor key, Nashville charts keep numbering from the parallel major, so the naturally lowered degrees are written with flats: the C minor progression Cm–A♭–E♭–B♭ is charted 1- ♭6 ♭3 ♭7.

How to practice: switch Notation to Nashville (names hidden) — the chart shows only numbers, and the current chord's name is revealed as it plays. Then change the key and notice the numbers don't change. When you can hear "1 5 6- 4" coming, you own the progression in all twelve keys.

Per-number accuracy (all time)

Four drills, one reflex

Number → Chord builds the lookup: hear "4 in the key of E" and reach for A without counting. Chord → Number reverses it, which is what you need when learning a song by ear. Chart reading strings numbers into real four-chord charts, one key at a time — the actual session-player skill. Progression by ear is the summit: no chart at all, just sound in, numbers out.

Keys are random on every question by design — the whole point of numbers is that the key doesn't matter. If you catch yourself translating through note names ("okay, 5 of A is… E"), that's normal at first; the drill exists to wear that middle step away.
